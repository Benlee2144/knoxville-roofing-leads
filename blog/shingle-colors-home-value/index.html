Do Shingle Colors Affect Home Value?

Updated October 2026 · 4 min read · By the Knoxville Roofing Quotes team

You're getting a new roof and suddenly you're picking from dozens of colors. Does it actually matter? Could you hurt your home's value by choosing wrong?

Color is rarely what makes or breaks a sale. Condition and age matter far more. But color does affect curb appeal, how well the house fits its street, and how hot the roof gets in summer.

What Buyers Notice First

We haven't found solid research showing that a particular roof color raises or lowers resale value. What buyers and inspectors do focus on:

  • Whether the roof is in good condition
  • How old the roof is
  • Whether it looks appropriate for the neighborhood

A roof in good shape in a sensible color beats an eye-catching color on a tired roof. If you're deciding whether to replace before selling, our guide to repair vs. replacement walks through it.

What Actually Matters for Curb Appeal

Harmony with Your House

The roof should complement your home's exterior – siding, brick, trim, stone. Not match exactly, but work together.

Traditional rule of thumb:

  • Warm house colors (tan, yellow, cream, red brick) → Warm roof colors (browns, warm grays)
  • Cool house colors (gray, blue, white) → Cool roof colors (gray, slate, charcoal)

But rules are meant to be broken. The key is that it shouldn't clash or look random.

Neighborhood Context

Drive through your neighborhood. What roof colors do you see? If every house has charcoal or brown roofs and you go bright red, your house will stick out – and not in a good way.

Many HOAs restrict roof colors or require architectural approval. Check before you order, and build any approval time into your schedule.

Architecture Style

Different home styles have traditional roof colors:

  • Colonial/Traditional: Dark gray, black, or dark brown
  • Craftsman: Earth tones, browns, greens
  • Mediterranean: Terra cotta, clay colors
  • Modern: Charcoal, black, flat gray
  • Ranch: Almost anything, but often browns or grays

Going with tradition isn't required, but it's safe.

The Energy Factor

Here's where color DOES make a measurable difference: energy efficiency.

Dark roofs absorb more of the sun's heat and get noticeably hotter on a summer afternoon, and Knoxville's July highs average in the upper 80s, according to NOAA's climate normals.

Light roofs reflect more heat and stay cooler, which can ease the load on your air conditioning.

How much that matters for your bills depends a lot on your attic insulation and ventilation, so a well-insulated, well-ventilated attic narrows the gap. Our guides to attic insulation and roof ventilation cover that side. Some manufacturers also make reflective shingles that stay cooler in darker colors; ask your roofer what's available.

Versatile Color Choices

These colors work with a wide range of homes:

  • Charcoal gray: The safe choice. Works with almost everything.
  • Weathered wood/brown: Popular with brick homes and traditional styles.
  • Black: Modern, bold, popular on newer construction.
  • Slate gray: A softer gray that works with many color schemes.
  • Driftwood/tan: Good for lighter colored homes.

Whatever color you choose, consider algae-resistant shingles. Those dark streaks on so many East Tennessee roofs are usually algae, which thrives in warm, humid weather. See our guide to black streaks on roofs.

Colors That Can Be Risky

Some colors are harder to pull off:

  • Bright blue: Very limiting for exterior colors
  • Green: Can look dated quickly or clash with landscaping
  • Red: Works on specific styles, looks odd on others
  • Light tan/beige: Shows dirt and staining more

These can work on the right house. But if you're worried about resale or want something safe, stick with grays and browns.

How to Choose

  1. Get samples. Most manufacturers offer sample boards. Look at them against your house in different lighting.
  2. Check at different times of day. Colors look different in morning vs evening light.
  3. Look at actual roofs. Drive around neighborhoods with similar homes. When you see a roof you like, note the color.
  4. Consider the long term. Architectural shingles typically last about 20 to 30 years, per InterNACHI and Modernize. A trendy color might feel dated well before then.
  5. Ask your roofer. They can show you photos or addresses of roofs they've done in the colors you're considering.

The Bottom Line

Unless you choose something really unusual, roof color won't significantly affect your home value. What matters is that the roof is quality, in good condition, and doesn't clash horribly with your house.

Pick a color you like that complements your home. Don't overthink it. In five years, you won't notice your roof color anymore – you'll just be glad it doesn't leak.
