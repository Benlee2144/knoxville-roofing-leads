If you own or manage a commercial building in Knoxville, your roof is probably nothing like the one on your house. Different materials, different problems, often different contractors. Here's what building owners should know before they collect bids.

Commercial vs. Residential: What's Different

Most commercial buildings have low-slope or flat roofs, while most houses have steep roofs with shingles. That changes a lot:

  • Drainage: Steep roofs shed water on their own. Flat roofs rely on drains, scuppers, gutters and tapered insulation to move it. With about 52 inches of precipitation in a normal year at Knoxville, according to the National Weather Service, drainage matters.
  • Materials: Shingles aren't made for flat roofs. Commercial roofs use membranes, built-up systems or, where there's enough slope, metal.
  • Foot traffic: HVAC technicians and other trades walk on commercial roofs regularly, which adds wear.
  • Scale and disruption: Bigger roofs mean bigger crews and equipment, and the work happens over an operating business.
  • Licensing: Tennessee's state contractor's license applies to projects of $25,000 or more. The Home Improvement license is for residential work, so it doesn't cover a storefront or office.

Common Commercial Roofing Systems

TPO (Thermoplastic Polyolefin)

A single-ply membrane, usually white, with heat-welded seams. It's widely used on new and replacement low-slope roofs. InterNACHI's life-expectancy chart gives TPO a broad range of 7 to 20 years; membrane thickness, installation quality and maintenance decide where a given roof lands.

EPDM (Rubber Roofing)

A black synthetic rubber membrane that's been used on low-slope roofs for decades. It can be fully adhered, mechanically fastened or ballasted with stone. InterNACHI estimates 15 to 25 years.

PVC (Polyvinyl Chloride)

Another heat-welded single-ply membrane, similar in appearance to TPO. It's often chosen where grease or chemicals may reach the roof, such as on restaurants, so ask the roofer whether it makes sense for your building.

Modified Bitumen

An asphalt-based sheet with added polymers, applied in layers by torch, hot asphalt or cold adhesive. InterNACHI puts it at about 20 years.

Built-Up Roofing (BUR)

The traditional "tar and gravel" roof: alternating layers of felt and asphalt, often topped with gravel. Heavy and durable. InterNACHI puts it at about 30 years.

Metal

Standing seam metal works on buildings with enough slope to shed water. It costs more up front, but InterNACHI's chart gives metal roofing a long life of 40 to 80 years.

Finding a Commercial Roofer

Not every roofer does commercial work. Here's how to narrow the field:

Check Their Experience

Ask about projects similar to yours in size and roof system, and get references from those building owners or managers.

Verify Licensing and Insurance

For projects of $25,000 or more, the roofer needs a Tennessee contractor's license, and each license carries a monetary limit, so confirm the limit covers your job. You can check a license on the state's online license search. For smaller commercial jobs, the state suggests asking your local codes office what's required. Ask every bidder for a certificate of insurance that names your company as the certificate holder.

Ask About Manufacturer Certifications

Membrane manufacturers certify contractors to install their systems, and a certification can be required for the manufacturer's longer system warranties. Ask which manufacturers each roofer is certified with.

Understand the Warranty

A manufacturer's system warranty, a materials-only warranty and the contractor's workmanship warranty are different documents. Ask what inspections and maintenance the manufacturer requires to keep coverage in force, and get everything in writing.

Common Commercial Roof Problems

Ponding Water

Water that's still standing a day or two after rain speeds up membrane wear and adds weight. It usually points to clogged drains, sagging deck or poor slope.

Membrane Punctures

Technicians walking to rooftop equipment can puncture a membrane. Walkway pads along regular service routes help.

Flashing Failures

Edges, penetrations, HVAC curbs and parapet walls are where many leaks start. Regular inspections catch these early. If you have an active leak, our roof leak repair page covers what to do first.

Storm Damage

Wind can lift membrane edges and seams, and hail can damage membranes and the insulation below. The National Weather Service office in Morristown notes that large hail around East Tennessee peaks in May and most summer severe weather is wind. See our storm damage page for next steps after a storm.

Maintenance Matters More

On a flat roof, small problems like a clogged drain or a small tear can turn into big ones quickly. Many building owners have a commercial roof checked in spring and fall and after major storms, with drains cleared and flashings looked over each time. A simple log of leaks, repairs and inspection photos helps with warranty claims and budgeting.

Repair, Restore or Replace?

A commercial roof doesn't always need a full tear-off. A localized leak can be patched, and a roof with dry, sound insulation may be a candidate for a coating or recover system. Wet insulation is often the deciding factor, so ask each roofer how they'll check for it. Re-roofing can also bring energy code requirements for roof insulation into play, depending on what your jurisdiction has adopted, so ask the roofer and your local codes office.

Planning for Replacement

Commercial replacements affect operations. Plan for:

  • Timing: Can work happen after hours or on weekends?
  • Noise and odors: Will installation disturb customers or employees?
  • Access: Will equipment block parking or entrances?
  • Phasing: Large roofs can be done in sections to limit disruption

Bottom Line for Building Owners

Your roof protects inventory, equipment and operations. Pick a contractor with commercial experience in your roof system, compare bids on the same scope, and budget for regular maintenance. It costs far less than water damage to a business.
